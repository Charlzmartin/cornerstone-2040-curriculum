CHAPTER ONE
A Steward's Declaration
“Unless the Lord builds the house, the builders labor in vain.” — Psalm 127:1, NIV
Thirty years is a long time when you have lived every day of it. When Cornerstone
Fellowship Church began, we could not see the people who would come through our
doors, the children who would grow up among us, or the difficult seasons we would face
together. We simply started walking, and the Lord has been faithful.
We have known joy and sorrow, growth and struggle, abundance and uncertainty. We
have worshiped, laughed, cried, and occasionally gotten on one another's nerves. That is
what families do. Yet we are still here, and more importantly, Christ is still here.
None of this happened because we possessed unusual wisdom or could anticipate every
turn. The Lord provided people, opened doors, corrected us, and carried us when our
strength was insufficient. Our history is not a record of human certainty. It is a testimony
to divine faithfulness.
I want to be careful with that statement. Jesus does not belong to Cornerstone.
Cornerstone belongs to Jesus.
This Church Belongs to Christ
Cornerstone Fellowship Church does not belong to its pastor, founding members,
deacons, teachers, musicians, or ministry leaders. We honor those who have prayed,
sacrificed, given, and served faithfully, but the church does not belong to any of us.
Jesus purchased His Church with His blood. He is its Head, Chief Shepherd, and
Cornerstone. If the church belongs to Christ, we are not owners. We are stewards.
An owner does what he wants with what belongs to him. A steward cares for what
belongs to another and uses it according to the owner's desires. For a season, Christ
entrusts His people, resources, ministries, and opportunities to pastors and faithful
servants. We preach, teach, lead, plan, and care for people, but none of it is ours.
Stewardship therefore reaches beyond finances. We steward doctrine by teaching it
faithfully, relationships by treating people with dignity, influence by using it to serve, and
opportunity by responding when Christ opens a door. We also steward the culture future
generations will inherit. Every decision says something about what we believe belongs at
the center.
That realization changes the way we hold everything.
Why Write This Now?
Why think about Cornerstone in 2040 when we have enough to address today? Because
thirty years has taught us that faithfulness cannot be assumed.
Every generation must decide whether it will follow Jesus, learn Scripture, and obey
Christ in its own circumstances. Every generation can also forget. We may preserve a
ministry after its purpose has disappeared or confuse a familiar method with the mission.
A church can remember its traditions and forget its Savior.
Methods that served us well may not serve the next generation in the same way.
Faithfulness does not require future leaders to reproduce every schedule, program, or

preference. It requires them to hold tightly to Christ, Scripture, discipleship, love, and
mission while exercising wisdom in their own time.
This manuscript is not an attempt to control Cornerstone's future. The world will change.
Buildings will age, technology will advance, methods will shift, pastors will come and go,
and people may forget our names. That is all right. Jesus Christ remains the same.
Our purpose is to help future leaders understand who we have learned we must be. We
want to pass on the convictions the Lord has formed through both joyful and difficult
lessons—not our personalities, preferences, or a museum of good memories, but
faithfulness to Jesus Christ.
Our Desire
Our desire is not merely that Cornerstone survives. We want this church to flourish in
faithfulness.
We want children not yet born to enter this fellowship, discover the beauty of Jesus, hear
the Gospel clearly, be loved deeply, and learn Scripture. We want them to discover their
gifts, serve others, endure hardship, repent when they stumble, and keep walking with
Christ. We want them to become mature disciples who help others follow Jesus—even if
they never know our names.
We want Cornerstone to be more than a building they visit. We want it to be a spiritual
family in which truth is spoken with love, prayer is a first response, service is normal, and
every generation helps another follow Jesus.
This work was never about us. It succeeds when people remember Jesus, even if they
forget us.
What We Are Praying For
We pray that future pastors will love Christ more than position and that every leader will
understand authority as a call to serve. We pray that truth will never be sacrificed for
popularity and that love will never be sacrificed in the name of being right.
We pray that children will know following Jesus as life's greatest privilege and that
members will understand discipleship as more than attending church. A disciple listens,
trusts, obeys, and returns to Christ after stumbling. We are not trying to manufacture
perfect people. We desire faithful people who keep walking with Jesus.
Our Commitment
Every pastor serves for a season. Every generation eventually places into other hands
what was once placed into theirs. The question is whether we will give them something
worth receiving.
With gratitude for those who laid the foundation and confidence in the Lord who
sustained us, we recommit ourselves to Cornerstone's stewardship. We will treasure
Christ, trust His Word, love people, tell the truth, serve generously, make disciples, and
develop leaders. We will hold our titles loosely and our calling tightly, preparing those
behind us to go farther than we have gone.
When our season is over, we will open our hands. Cornerstone belonged to Jesus thirty
years ago. It belongs to Him today. It will belong to Him in 2040 and beyond.
We are not owners. We are stewards. May we be found faithful until He comes, for His
glory alone.

CHAPTER TWO
The Cornerstone Identity
“You also, like living stones, are being built into a spiritual house to be a holy
priesthood...” — 1 Peter 2:5, NIV
Every church has an identity. Sometimes it is carefully defined; sometimes it develops as
people worship, serve, struggle, and grow together. Over time, certain convictions and
ways of relating become normal.
Cornerstone has a distinct story and personality, but our identity cannot rest upon a
pastor, worship style, favorite ministry, or shared history. Those things help tell our story,
but they cannot carry the weight of who we are.
Our identity is found in Jesus Christ.
Cornerstone Fellowship Church is a spiritual family of passionate followers of Jesus,
redeemed by God's grace, united by the Gospel, and empowered by the Holy Spirit for
God's glory. We come from different families, experiences, backgrounds, and
personalities, yet we belong to one another because we first belong to Him.
Jesus Christ is our Cornerstone. That is more than our name. It is who we are.
Christ Is Our Treasure
I love Cornerstone's fellowship, worship, laughter, and the moments when someone
begins to understand God's Word. But it is possible to love church and lose sight of
Christ. Songs, preaching, programs, relationships, and memories are good gifts; the
danger comes when the gifts become more precious than the Giver.
Jesus is our treasure. We rejoice in His Cross and resurrection, depend upon Him as
Savior and King, and await His return. The Gospel is not merely the message that saved
us years ago. It is the good news in which we live every day.
Christ remains our hope when worship is joyful and when life is painful, when the church
is growing and when ministry is difficult. He is not one value among many. Every other
value receives its meaning from Him.
We preach Christ because there is no better message, worship Him because no one is
more worthy, and obey Him because we trust Him.
At the center is Christ.
Always Christ.
God's Word Is Our Foundation
Because Jesus is our Cornerstone, we take seriously the Word He has given us. We
believe Scripture is the inspired, authoritative, and sufficient Word of God. Through it,
God reveals Himself, exposes our sin, confronts our thinking, points us to Christ, and
equips us for faithful living.
Scripture must shape our preaching, teaching, leadership, ministries, and decisions. It
must also shape our marriages, money, relationships, attitudes, and the way we treat
people when nobody from church is watching.

This means we do not use Scripture merely to confirm what we already prefer. We allow
it to correct us. We study it carefully, teach it in context, and submit our personal
opinions to its authority. Truth is not always comfortable, but it is always necessary for a
church that wants to remain faithful.
Culture and popular opinion will change. If we build around every shifting idea, we will
spend our lives moving the furniture. God has spoken. His Word is our foundation.
We Are Disciples
A disciple is a follower of Jesus Christ. Discipleship is not a class, program, certificate, or
accumulation of biblical information. Those things may help us, but discipleship is a life.
It is the lifelong process of hearing God's Word, believing Him, obeying Him, and
becoming more like Jesus. Therefore, discipleship reaches into how we worship, work,
parent, forgive, serve, think, desire, give, speak, and respond when life hurts or we do not
get our way.
Disciples do not mature in a straight line. We pass through seasons of growth, waiting,
service, suffering, and renewed obedience. We stumble, repent, receive grace, and
continue walking. Maturity is not the absence of struggle; it is a growing willingness to
remain with Christ through every season.
Church activity can fool us. We may know when to stand, clap, and say amen while
resisting Christ in daily life. Spiritual maturity is not measured only by what we know, but
by whether we are learning to obey the One we know.
We Are Family
God did not save us to live the Christian life alone. He joined us to a body and gave us
brothers and sisters in Christ.
Family life is not always easy. We disagree, misunderstand, and sometimes hurt one
another. Yet relationships become one of God's primary places of formation. At
Cornerstone, we cultivate love, encouragement, accountability, forgiveness, hospitality,
and mutual care. We celebrate and grieve together, bear burdens, and lovingly challenge
one another.
Real community requires patience and the humility to admit when we are wrong. It
means loving people who are still growing while remembering that we are still growing
too. Unity is not pretending differences do not exist. It is choosing to handle them
beneath the lordship of Christ.
No one walks alone—not because the church will meet every need perfectly, but because
Christ has joined us together and often uses His people to shape His people.
We Are Servants
Jesus, who deserved to be served by everyone, came serving. We cannot follow Him while
spending our lives waiting for others to serve us.
Service is not an occasional activity. God has given every believer gifts, abilities,
resources, influence, and time for the good of others and His glory. Some service is
visible; much of it is known only to the person helped and the Lord. Both matter.
We serve our congregation, neighbors, and community, including people who may never
join Cornerstone or repay us. We serve because Jesus served us. Compassion may open a
door, but Christ transforms lives.

Service also protects us from becoming consumers who judge the church mainly by what
it provides for us. The question of a disciple is not only, “What did I receive?” but also,
“Whom did Christ give me an opportunity to serve?”
We Are Generous
Everything we possess was entrusted to us by God. Our time, abilities, influence,
opportunities, and resources belong beneath the lordship of Christ. Generosity is
therefore more than what we place in an offering. It is a life lived with open hands.
We give to strengthen the Church, advance the Gospel, and care for people in need. Our
desire is not merely to fund ministry but to become generous people.
Generosity is expressed through money, but also through attention, hospitality,
encouragement, knowledge, and time. Open hands reveal trust in the God who first gave
Himself to us.
We Are Multipliers
The Christian life was never intended to terminate with us. Someone taught, prayed for,
encouraged, or patiently walked with us. Now it is our turn.
Healthy disciples begin influencing others. Parents teach children, older believers
encourage younger ones, friends walk with friends, and teachers open Scripture.
Disciples become disciple-makers; servants mature into leaders; leaders develop other
leaders.
Multiplication usually begins quietly. A conversation continues after Bible study.
Someone invites another believer to pray. A mature saint shares wisdom earned through
hardship. These ordinary relationships become the pathways through which faith travels
from one generation to another.
This is why we invest in people at every stage—from seekers and new believers to mature
servants and emerging leaders. We especially invest in children, students, and young
adults. They are not merely the church of tomorrow. They are worshiping, learning,
serving, and growing as Christ's Church today.
Success is not measured by how many people gather around one gifted leader, but by
how many disciples are equipped to help someone else follow Jesus.
This identity must be more than language on a page. It should be recognizable in our
priorities, budgets, calendars, leadership practices, and relationships. What we
repeatedly do will teach future generations what we truly value.
This Is Who We Are
Cornerstone Fellowship Church is a Christ-centered family of disciple-making disciples
who treasure Jesus, stand upon God's Word, pursue joyful obedience, love one another,
serve generously, and raise faithful leaders for God's glory.
We are not competing with another church or measuring our worth by attendance,
buildings, budgets, or visibility. We want to know Christ, trust Him, follow Him, and
become increasingly translucent so others see less of us and more of Him. Then we want
to help those walking behind us do the same.
At the center is Christ.
Always Christ.

CHAPTER THREE
Our Mission
“As the Father has sent me, I am sending you.” — John 20:21, NIV
Why does Cornerstone Fellowship Church exist?
Churches do many good and necessary things: worship, preach, teach, pray, counsel, feed
people, visit the sick, strengthen families, baptize believers, and attend more meetings
than we imagined possible. Yet activity alone does not define our mission. A church can
be busy and still lose sight of why Jesus left us here.
Cornerstone exists to glorify God by helping people know, trust, and follow Jesus Christ—
and faithfully lead others to do the same. Ministries and methods will change, but the
mission must remain clear.
A simple question can guide our decisions: Does this help people take their next faithful
step with Christ? Not every ministry will look alike or produce immediate results, but
everything should serve the mission Christ has given His Church.
We are not here merely to maintain an institution. We are here to make disciples.
We Invite People to Christ
Every disciple's journey begins with God's grace. Christ meets us in our need and calls us
to Himself.
People come to Cornerstone from many places. Some are searching, skeptical, wounded,
disappointed, angry, or simply curious. Others enter because someone cared enough to
say, “Come go with me.” We welcome them because every person bears God's image and
is someone for whom Christ died.
Welcome does not mean avoiding truth or pretending every belief is equally faithful. It
means receiving people with dignity, listening to their stories, and making room for them
to encounter Jesus without requiring them to become polished before they enter.
Our first responsibility is not to convince people that Cornerstone is wonderful. It is to
introduce them to Jesus Christ. He is the Savior we proclaim, the hope we offer, and the
life we have found.
At the center is Christ.
Always Christ.
We Form Disciples, Not Spectators
Coming to Christ begins the journey. Attendance matters because gathering with God's
people matters, but “sitting in a worship service does not automatically produce maturity
any more than sitting in a garage makes someone a Buick.” Old joke. Still true.
Making disciples means helping people hear God's Word, believe Him, pray, worship, live
in biblical community, pursue holiness, discover their gifts, and obey Jesus. Biblical
knowledge matters, but information must become transformation. The Word must travel
from the page into the person, shaping how we think, speak, forgive, spend, love, serve,
and respond when life does not go our way.

This formation takes time. New believers need patient instruction. Growing believers
need relationships that encourage both grace and accountability. Mature disciples need
opportunities to serve, lead, and pour into others. The church must create a clear path
without pretending that spiritual growth can be completed like a course.
Our aim is not merely informed minds. It is transformed lives.
We Serve as Jesus Served
Jesus never separated compassion from truth. He proclaimed the Kingdom, fed hungry
people, touched those others avoided, confronted sin, and wept with grieving friends.
We want that same spirit at Cornerstone. We feed the hungry, comfort the hurting,
encourage the lonely, counsel the discouraged, and strengthen families because love
must become visible. Compassion is not a substitute for the Gospel, but neither should we
tell a hungry person Jesus loves them while refusing to share the sandwich in our hand.
We serve people, not projects. Those receiving care are never props for publicity or
numbers for a report. They are neighbors to be treated with the same dignity we would
desire in our own season of need.
We serve because Christ served us. Compassion may open doors; Christ transforms lives.
We Develop Disciple-Makers
As disciples mature, they begin looking beyond themselves. A parent considers a child's
spiritual life. An older believer encourages a younger one. A friend notices another
friend's questions. Someone realizes, “A person helped me. Perhaps I can help someone
else.”
That is how multiplication begins. Parents disciple children, friends walk with friends,
and leaders develop future leaders. This work is not reserved for pastors or people with
titles. Every healthy disciple should eventually ask, “Who am I helping follow Jesus?”
Disciple-making does not always require a formal program. It may happen over a meal,
during a hospital visit, in a weekly telephone call, or through a faithful friendship. What
matters is the intentional sharing of truth, life, prayer, and obedience.
Even difficult experiences may become places from which we encourage others. God
wastes nothing. Our prayer is that every Cornerstone generation will help form another
generation of faithful followers of Christ.
We Live Sent
Ministry does not happen mainly inside the church building. After worship, we scatter
into homes, workplaces, classrooms, neighborhoods, stores, hospitals, and ordinary
conversations. Those places matter.
Jesus sends every believer. We carry His presence and message into daily life,
representing Him in how we speak, work, forgive, serve, and love. When God opens the
door, we clearly tell people about the hope we have found in Christ.
Our homes are places of mission. Our workplaces and neighborhoods are places of
mission. Even interruptions may become holy opportunities when we remain attentive to
what Christ is doing around us.
We do not merely attend church. We are Christ's Church.

Our Mission
Cornerstone Fellowship Church exists to glorify God by leading people to believe in Jesus,
grow as obedient disciples, and multiply disciple-makers who represent Him faithfully in
every sphere of life.
This mission must guide our ministries, leadership development, stewardship, and
preparation of future generations. Wise leaders may change methods that no longer serve
the mission, but they must not lose the mission.
Help people believe in Jesus. Help believers grow as disciples. Help disciples become
disciple-makers. Wherever Christ sends us, represent Him faithfully.
At the center is Christ.
Always Christ.

CHAPTER FOUR
Our Vision
“He who began a good work in you will carry it on to completion until the day of Christ
Jesus.” — Philippians 1:6, NIV
After thirty years of pastoring Cornerstone, I often wonder what this church will become.
Who will lead ten or twenty years from now? What will worship look like? What methods
and technologies will future generations use?
I do not know, and vision is not the ability to predict every detail. It is not Christian
fortune-telling with a Bible verse attached. God alone knows the full measure of His
purpose. Yet He calls us to walk by faith, labor with expectation, and prepare for the
work He intends to accomplish.
Our vision is not human ambition. We are not imagining the largest crowd or most
impressive ministry we can build. We are asking a more important question: What kind of
people is Christ forming us to become?
We believe God is shaping Cornerstone into a Christ-centered community that reflects
His Son, proclaims His Gospel, and develops generations of disciples who joyfully obey
Him. We cannot name every future ministry, but we know who must remain at the center.
At the center is Christ.
Always Christ.
We See a Church That Treasures Christ
A person can know much about Jesus without treasuring Jesus. Biblical knowledge, sound
doctrine, church attendance, and religious language do not necessarily reveal what the
heart loves.
We envision a church where truth about Christ leads to deeper affection for Christ. Jesus
is not merely the subject of our preaching; He is the object of our affection. As He
becomes more precious, preferences matter less, recognition loses its appeal, and
possessions loosen their grip.
This does not mean living in permanent spiritual excitement. Emotions change. We will
have days of joy and days of distraction, weariness, or struggle. Beneath them all,
however, should grow a settled conviction: I have found my treasure in Christ.
Our success is not measured only by attendance or activity, but by whether Jesus is
becoming increasingly precious to us.
We See a Church of Authentic Disciples
If discipleship is a life, its greatest evidence will not be found on Sunday morning alone. It
will appear on Monday—in marriages and families, business decisions and finances,
private habits and difficult conversations. God measures spirituality where life happens.
We envision husbands and wives living faithfully before Christ, parents teaching children,
and children learning to know Jesus for themselves. We see integrity governing work,
forgiveness replacing bitterness, neighbors receiving love, and holiness pursued with
humility.

We obey because we believe Jesus. We trust His commands, wisdom, promises, and
grace. Authentic disciples are not perfect people. They are people who keep learning,
surrendering, repenting, receiving grace, and walking with Christ.
Cornerstone's greatest testimony will not be its buildings or programs. It will be the
Christlike character of its people.
We See a Church That Walks Together
We need one another. Cornerstone should be a fellowship where no one is expected to
walk alone. New believers are nurtured, families strengthened, older adults honored,
children cherished, students challenged, and young adults equipped to follow Christ in
their world.
We envision friendships deepening, differences being reconciled, burdens being shared,
and prayer becoming natural because we know one another well. Hospitality means
opening our homes, tables, and lives.
Relationships will become messy because church people are still people. We will say the
wrong thing, misunderstand one another, and expose places where sanctification is still
needed. But we do not run at the first sign of difficulty. We talk, listen, forgive, repent,
and extend the grace we have received.
Biblical community is one way Christ forms His people. Sometimes the person who
irritates us reveals something in us that still needs surrender. I do not particularly enjoy
that truth either, but it remains true.
No one walks alone.
We See a Church That Serves with Extraordinary Compassion
We want Cornerstone to be known in its community—not merely for its name, building,
preaching, music, or food, though we know something about food. We want people to
know that Cornerstone loves people.
We envision a church that listens before speaking, serves without waiting to be asked,
welcomes without partiality, and gives without requiring recognition. We feed hungry
people, comfort those who grieve, strengthen families, and offer hope to the discouraged.
Christian compassion also shares the Gospel with gentleness and conviction because it
does not ignore anyone's deepest need for Christ. Jesus noticed people others passed,
touched those others avoided, welcomed children, ate with sinners, and stopped for
hurting people while on His way somewhere else.
We want neighbors to experience Christ's love through Cornerstone before entering our
sanctuary. Then, when God opens the door, we tell them why: we love because Christ first
loved us.
We See a Church That Raises Leaders
Leadership in Christ's Church does not begin with a title or empty position. It begins with
discipleship. A believer learns to trust and obey Jesus, begins serving, helps someone else
follow Christ, and gradually develops character, gifts, and influence. A leader emerges.
Not every mature believer will hold office, but mature disciples influence others. They
serve, encourage, teach, mentor, and lead because the life of Christ in them is no longer
turned inward.

Leadership development cannot wait until a vacancy appears. By then, we are late. Every
ministry should cultivate future servants and shepherds: children growing into disciples,
disciples becoming disciple-makers, and servant leaders investing in those behind them.
We do not want to spend the future desperately filling positions. We want to spend the
present developing people.
We See a Church That Extends Christ's Kingdom
Cornerstone has never existed only for Cornerstone. Christ is building His Church around
the world, and we have the privilege of joining His work.
We envision strengthening churches, encouraging pastors, supporting missionaries, and
partnering with ministries that proclaim the Gospel and serve people faithfully. Some
seeds we plant may bear fruit we never see. That is all right. Someone planted seeds that
became Cornerstone; we can plant for others.
Whether across the street or across the world, we rejoice whenever Christ is glorified.
His Kingdom does not need our name printed upon everything we touch.
We See a Church That Outlives Its Leaders
This vision is personal for me. I have loved, prayed for, worried about, and rejoiced over
Cornerstone for thirty years. Yet I am a steward. My assignment has a beginning and an
end, as does every pastor's. Jesus is Cornerstone's ultimate, true, and final Leader.
We envision future generations receiving a culture rooted in Scripture, centered upon
Christ, committed to discipleship, and devoted to God's glory. We hope they change
methods that no longer work, use tools we have not imagined, and develop ministries that
would never occur to us. We pray they follow Christ courageously in their world.
But we pray they never lose Jesus.
If they treasure Christ, stand upon His Word, love one another, serve generously, make
disciples, and prepare another generation, our stewardship will have succeeded—not
because they preserved us, but because they remained faithful to Him.
We cannot see every detail of the future. We know the One who is already there. He
began this work, has sustained it, and will carry it toward completion.
At the center is Christ.
Always Christ.

CHAPTER FIVE
Our Core Values
“Whatever you do, whether in word or deed, do it all in the name of the Lord Jesus.” —
Colossians 3:17, NIV
Every church has values. The question is whether its true values are printed in a
document or practiced by its people.
Real values appear in what we protect, celebrate, fund, and sacrifice. They guide us when
decisions are difficult and when good opportunities compete for limited time, money, or
attention. Cornerstone's core values are biblical convictions intended to govern how we
worship, lead, serve, decide, and live together. They are our attempt to express what
matters to Christ.
We Treasure Jesus Christ
Jesus Christ is the center of everything. He is not merely the Church's Founder or a name
in our songs. He is our Savior, Shepherd, King, Head, and Cornerstone. Every ministry
should help people know, love, trust, and follow Him.
This value becomes real when Christ's purpose competes with our preferences, obedience
costs comfort, or service brings no recognition. A treasure is something we consider
worth more than something else. We treasure Christ above our pride, plans, reputation,
and comfort.
At the center is Christ.
Always Christ.
We Submit to God's Word
After more than forty years of preaching, I still discover more in Scripture. Familiar
passages arrest my attention, open new doors, and challenge me again. That is the Bible.
We believe Scripture is inspired, trustworthy, authoritative, and sufficient. It is our final
authority for faith and life. We measure our beliefs, ministries, and priorities against
God's Word rather than culture or personal preference.
Not every decision has a verse attached. Scripture does not prescribe the hour of worship
or which technology to purchase, but it shapes the wisdom and character with which we
decide. When Scripture speaks clearly, our responsibility is to listen.
The question is not merely, What do I think? or What is everyone else doing? We ask,
What has God said? We remain under the Word, allowing it to comfort, confront, confirm,
and correct us.
We Believe Before We Obey
Biblical obedience begins deeper than a list of rules. It begins with belief.
We do not obey to earn Christ's love. We obey because we believe Him. We believe He is
who He says He is, His Word is true, His promises are trustworthy, His commands are
good, and His grace is sufficient. Because we believe, we trust; because we trust, we
obey.

Our disobedience often exposes a question of belief. Do I trust His wisdom more than
mine? Do I believe He sees what I cannot? We rarely announce that we do not trust Jesus.
We simply do what we want and call it something else.
Obedience tells the truth about trust. It is not a miserable obligation but an act of
worship that declares Jesus worthy of our confidence.
We Stay with Christ
Following Jesus is not a trial arrangement we reconsider when life becomes difficult. It is
a lifelong relationship in which we learn to remain through every season.
When life is joyful, stay with Christ. When grief enters, prayers seem unanswered, or
obedience becomes costly, stay. We remain not because it is always easy, but because
Peter's question remains ours: “Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal
life.” Peter did not understand everything. He knew whom he was staying with.
Transformation is not manufactured by trying harder to look Christian. It is Christ's
gracious work in those who abide in Him. Faithfulness is refusing to leave Christ when
difficulty comes.
Stay with Christ.
We Grow Together
People are complicated. I am one of them, and so are you. That knowledge should help us
extend grace.
God joins us to a body where brothers and sisters encourage, challenge, pray for, and
sometimes reveal how much sanctification we still need. We pursue relationships marked
by truth, love, accountability, forgiveness, humility, and generosity.
Community requires intention. Someone must make the call, open the door, ask the
second question after “I'm fine,” and listen without immediately trying to fix everything.
Sometimes love must also say, “My brother, my sister, you are wrong.”
Healthy relationships require both truth and love. No one walks alone.
We Serve Generously
Jesus came not to be served but to serve. The One who deserved everything took a towel
and washed feet, then told us to follow Him. Service cannot be beneath us.
We use our gifts, opportunities, and resources for the good of others and God's glory. We
serve members, neighbors, strangers, and communities near and far—not only when
service is convenient or visible. If Christ is glorified and people are helped, that is

Generosity flows from understanding how generously Christ has treated us. We received
grace we could not earn, mercy we did not deserve, and life purchased at a price we
could not pay. Generous people reflect a generous Savior.

What Christ is doing in us should eventually reach someone else. Healthy disciples
become disciple-makers; disciple-makers become servant leaders; and servant leaders
develop others. This is not a corporate pipeline. It is spiritual life multiplying through
relationships.

CHAPTER SIX
The Cornerstone Path
“I am the way and the truth and the life. No one comes to the Father except through
me.” — John 14:6, NIV
The Christian life does not move in a straight line. We do not come to Christ, complete a
few lessons, overcome every weakness, and steadily rise until a certificate announces that
we have arrived.
Life with Christ has seasons. Sometimes growth seems visible, Scripture feels alive,
prayer is rich, and obedience is clear. Other times we wait, struggle, serve, suffer, or do
several of those at once. Through every season, Christ is transforming us into His image.
This lifelong journey with Christ is what Cornerstone calls the Cornerstone Path. It is not
a ladder of spiritual achievement. It is a way of understanding faithful discipleship
through changing seasons.
The Path Is Christ
The Path is not a program, curriculum, list of classes, or system by which we rank
spiritual maturity. Curriculum can help us understand it, but the Path is Christ.
Jesus did not merely say He would show us the way. He said, “I am the way.” Christianity
is not primarily a set of principles to master; it is relationship with a living Person. The
Christian life is not a journey toward Christ as though He waits at the finish line. It is a
journey with Christ.
He is our beginning and end, Way and Companion, Teacher and Strength, Shepherd and
Hope. Therefore, the central question is not, “How far have I come compared with
someone else?” Comparison produces pride or discouragement while turning our eyes
away from Jesus.
A better question is: What does faithfulness to Christ look like in this season of my
journey?
You Are Here
Most of us have stood before a large map searching for the marker that makes the rest of
it useful: YOU ARE HERE. A map cannot guide us until we recognize where we are.
The same is true spiritually. We want every disciple to recognize the present season—not
for ranking or comparison, but for honest response to Christ. You may be learning to
believe Jesus, facing an area of long-resisted obedience, growing quietly, waiting,
suffering, or receiving an opportunity to serve.
You are here, and Christ meets you here. We often wish we were somewhere else, want a
trial to end, or compare our journey with another person's. But the question is not why
your life does not look like theirs. It is: How is Christ inviting you to trust and obey Him
here?

Belief Is the Beginning of Obedience
Discipleship begins with belief—not belief in ourselves, but in Jesus Christ. We believe
His death is sufficient, His resurrection conquered death, His Word is true, His promises
trustworthy, His commands good, and His grace sufficient.
Because we believe Him, we trust Him. Because we trust Him, we obey Him.
Many struggles with obedience reveal a struggle with belief. When I refuse to forgive, do
I believe Jesus knows what He is asking? When I grip what He tells me to release, do I
trust Him with what happens next? When I choose my way, have I decided my wisdom is
better than His?
Our obedience does not earn God's love. We obey because the Cross has already revealed
that love. Belief gives birth to obedience.
Growth Is Often Hidden
Spiritual growth can be frustrating because we cannot always see it. It resembles roots
extending beneath the soil. People admire branches and fruit; nobody applauds roots. Yet
the tree is being strengthened before evidence appears above ground.
Much of Christ's work happens where nobody sees it. We believe His Word, obey in a
small matter, repent, forgive, wait, resist temptation, pray without feeling like it, and
persevere. Day after day, Christ shapes us quietly and sometimes invisibly.
I understand this through my lifelong adventures with weight loss. You do the right thing
for three days and step on the scale expecting a parade. The scale congratulates you by
doing absolutely nothing. Still, something may be happening.
Most of life with Christ occurs on ordinary Tuesdays and Thursdays, not mountaintops.
Consistency is key. Faithfulness in ordinary places gives Christ room to do extraordinary
work.
Transformation Is Revealed
Then the season changes. A trial comes, disappointment knocks, temptation returns,
someone hurts us, or an inconvenient opportunity to serve appears. Such moments do not
create character as much as reveal what Christ has been forming.
Will I trust, obey, forgive, serve, and stay?
Transformation Revealed is not another level on the Path. It is the gracious work of Christ
becoming visible in daily life. Perhaps criticism once produced anger, but now you listen.
Bitterness once ruled, but now you forgive. Fear once controlled your decisions, but now
you obey. Suffering once made you run, but through tears and questions, you remain.
You may not notice the change at first, but Christ has been at work. Transformation is
revealed along the way.
Stay with Christ
This is the heartbeat of the Cornerstone Path: Stay with Christ.
Jesus calls disciples to abide—not merely visit Him during trouble or admire Him from a
religious distance. When we rejoice, grieve, understand, struggle, wait, or find obedience
costly, we remain.

CHAPTER SEVEN
The Fruit of the Path
“This is to my Father's glory, that you bear much fruit, showing yourselves to be my

What Does a Mature Disciple Look Like?
Is maturity measured by years in church, Scripture knowledge, position, or knowing
exactly when to say amen? Those things may reflect growth, but none guarantees it. A
person can master church language while resisting Jesus where He calls for trust and
obedience.
Jesus said a tree is known by its fruit. If the Path is Christ, its fruit is Christ becoming
increasingly visible in us. This transformation is the Holy Spirit's work. We cannot
manufacture Christlikeness, yet grace does not make us passive. We hear, believe, obey,
and remain in Christ as the Spirit works within us.
The fruit of discipleship is not perfection but increasing resemblance to Jesus. Mature
disciples have not arrived. They are becoming faithful, teachable, responsive, and willing
to let Christ rule every part of life.
We Treasure Christ Above Ourselves
Mature disciples increasingly love Christ more than comfort, recognition, possessions,
ambition, and preference. That becomes revealing when His will interrupts our plans or
leads along a road we would not choose.
We treasure Jesus not only for what He gives, but for who He is: Savior, Shepherd, King,
Wisdom, Righteousness, and Life. In Him we have not merely received blessings; we have
been brought home to God.
As Christ becomes more precious, everything else finds its proper place. We enjoy gifts
without worshiping them, hold plans without being controlled by them, and release
preferences without losing peace. The mature disciple is learning to say, “Jesus, You are
worth more.”
We Believe Before We Obey
Every act of obedience rests upon faith. We obey not to earn Christ's love but because we
have already been loved. We follow even when a command is difficult or an outcome
unclear because Jesus is trustworthy.
Obedience is faith made visible. When Christ tells us to forgive, give, wait, speak truth,
love an enemy, or take an unexplained step, the deeper question is: Do I trust Him?
Mature disciples learn to answer yes—not perfectly, without questions, or always without
tears, but yes. Faith gives birth to obedience, and obedience deepens faith as we discover
again that Christ was trustworthy all along.
We Practice Lifelong Repentance
Repentance is not only the doorway into Christian life. It is the continuing posture of
growing disciples. As Christ reveals more of Himself, His light exposes attitudes, motives,
habits, and wounds we have excused or hidden.

A church that cannot repent cannot mature. A church whose people humbly confess,

We Become Increasingly Translucent
Discipleship is not self-improvement. It is Christ revealed.

not that people admire us, but that they encounter Jesus through us.
We Love Sacrificially

Sacrificial love may cost time, pride, money, convenience, or the right to have the last

The mature disciple increasingly asks, “What would love require of me here?” Then, by
grace, acts upon the answer.
We Serve Joyfully

CHAPTER EIGHT
Released to Ministry
“Now to each one the manifestation of the Spirit is given for the common good.” — 1

Ministry does not belong only to pastors. Scripture describes the Church as a body
composed of many parts, each with a function. The eye sees, the ear hears, the hand
grasps, and the foot gets us where we need to go. Different functions, one body.
Every follower of Jesus has been gifted by the Holy Spirit for the common good and the
building of the Body. We will not serve in the same ways or places, but no believer is
unnecessary or without purpose.
There are no spectators in the Body of Christ.
Every Believer Has a Ministry
Some people preach, teach, sing, or lead publicly. Others make calls, prepare meals,
organize materials, give generously, pray faithfully, or sit quietly with someone who
hurts. Which ministry matters more? That is the wrong question.
If the Spirit has gifted a believer and that gift is used faithfully for others and Christ's
glory, the ministry matters. Visibility must never be confused with value. The person
holding a microphone is not more important than the person praying in the back of the
room.
There is a place for every member in the work of Christ—not merely because a volunteer
schedule has openings, but because the Spirit has chosen to work through His people. If
you belong to Christ, you have been called to serve.
The Holy Spirit Gives the Gifts
Spiritual gifts are not earned, purchased, or awarded to the most impressive. The Holy
Spirit distributes them according to His wisdom.
Our responsibility is not to manufacture gifts or force everyone toward the roles we
recognize most easily. We pay attention. What burden has God placed in a person's
heart? Where does that individual bear fruit? What ability repeatedly strengthens others?
Where does God's grace seem to work through this person?
We recognize, encourage, cultivate, and, at the right time, release people to serve. Every
ministry begins with God's initiative; our role is faithful stewardship.
Gifts Before Titles
Church people can become preoccupied with titles. We may focus upon what someone is
called rather than what God has gifted that person to do.
At Cornerstone, we first ask, “How has the Spirit gifted this person?” If someone teaches,
encourages, shows mercy, leads, gives, serves, or proclaims truth, we help that gift
become faithful and fruitful.
The work comes before recognition. A title should acknowledge what the Spirit is already
accomplishing through a faithful servant. Function comes before titles because our desire
is not to create positions but to release ministry.

CHAPTER NINE
Leadership as Servanthood
“Whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant.” — Matthew 20:26,
NIV
People often desire leadership before understanding its cost. We see the person making
decisions, influencing others, or carrying a title. We do not always see late-night calls,
difficult conversations, private prayers, criticism, disappointment, and the weight of
caring for people. The brochure leaves a few things out.
Jesus never hid the cost. When James and John sought prominence, He contrasted worldly
rulers who “lord it over” others with Kingdom leadership: “Not so with you.” Those words
should remain in every Christian leader's heart.
Jesus came down, took the nature of a servant, and humbled Himself to the Cross. His
way becomes ours. The towel comes before the title.
Leadership Begins at the Feet of Jesus
Before leading people, we follow Christ. Responsibilities can make leaders so busy
working for Jesus that they stop sitting with Jesus. A leader who no longer listens to
Christ eventually leads from experience, instinct, personality, pressure, or yesterday's
strength.
Functioning is not the same as following.
Mary sat at Jesus' feet. The disciples left their nets. Peter learned that loving Christ came
before shepherding Christ's sheep. Leadership begins by listening, learning, and
following.
Before asking, “Where am I leading these people?” we ask, “Am I still following Jesus?”
We Follow Before We Lead
No leader graduates from discipleship—not the pastor, deacon, teacher, or ministry
leader. Greater responsibility should deepen our awareness of our need to remain with
Christ.
After more than forty years of preaching, I am still learning. Christ continues correcting
attitudes, confronting fears, and identifying areas where He is still working on me. That is
not a leadership disqualification. Refusing to listen may be.
Faithful leaders remain teachable. They receive correction, admit mistakes, and change
direction when Scripture and wisdom make change necessary. We do not have to pretend
we know everything because someone gave us a title. We follow before we lead, and
while leading, we keep following.
Character Is Our Greatest Credential
Gifts impress quickly; character takes longer to see. A person may speak well, organize
effectively, or draw attention, but gifts cannot carry leadership's full weight.
Character appears under criticism, when someone else is recognized, when a decision
goes another way, or when nobody watches. This is why Scripture emphasizes self-
control, faithfulness, hospitality, gentleness, integrity, and healthy relationships.

Healthy leaders do not gather ministry around themselves. They release ministry through

CHAPTER TEN
A Culture of Truth and Grace
“Speaking the truth in love, we will grow to become in every respect the mature body of
him who is the head, that is, Christ.” — Ephesians 4:15, NIV
Truth and love belong together, though the Church has not always kept them together
well. Some Christians hold truth firmly but deliver it harshly. Others fear causing
discomfort and avoid necessary truth.
Jesus was full of grace and truth—not half of each, but full of both. He refused to
condemn the woman caught in adultery and told her to leave her life of sin. He loved the
wealthy young man and told him what he did not want to hear. He welcomed sinners and
never pretended sin did not matter.
We desire Cornerstone to become a people who tell truth because we love one another
and love one another enough to tell truth. Truth without love can become a weapon. Love
without truth can become a lie.
We Tell the Truth
Honesty sounds simple until it costs us. Truth may require an uncomfortable
conversation, admitting we were wrong, revealing a struggle, or risking another person's
disappointment.
So we hide. We say “I'm fine” while falling apart, smile in the hallway while carrying
resentment, remain silent in meetings, and discuss concerns with everyone except the
person who needs to hear them. Church people can appear peaceful while privately
fighting wars. That is not peace.
At Cornerstone, we reject gossip, manipulation, and the quiet dishonesty of pretense. This
does not authorize everyone to say whatever comes to mind. Wisdom, timing, and motive
matter. But when truth needs to be spoken, we speak it because relationships built upon
pretense cannot become healthy.
We Tell the Truth in Love
How we speak matters. Cruelty does not become righteous because someone says, “I'm
just being honest.” A person can be honest and mean.
Love governs the motive, tone, and purpose of truth. Before correcting someone, we ask

Prayer as Dependence
“Apart from me you can do nothing.” — John 15:5, NIV
I have prayed for many years, though not always consistently or with great faith. Some
seasons prayer felt as natural as breathing. In others my mind wandered, words felt dry,
and I wondered whether I was merely repeating yesterday.
Still, Christ kept calling me back.
Prayer is not simply something Christians do. It is the confession of a dependent life.
Every genuine prayer acknowledges what our flesh prefers to forget: We need God—not
only when the doctor calls, money is short, or someone we love is in trouble, but when life
is going well, ministry is flourishing, and we know exactly what we intend to do next.
Perhaps especially then.
Jesus said, “Apart from me you can do nothing.” We believe Him, so we pray.
The Breath of the Church
Prayer is not one ministry among many. It is the life of every ministry. We preach
prayerfully because only God opens hearts, teach prayerfully because information cannot
transform, serve prayerfully because compassion cannot give eternal life, and lead
prayerfully because we cannot see tomorrow.
Planning is not the problem. The problem begins when confidence in our plan becomes
greater than dependence upon God. Every genuine work of God begins with His initiative
and is sustained by His power.
Prayer is not our last resort after ideas are exhausted. It is our first response. In
opportunity, difficulty, uncertainty, blessing, conflict, growth, and loss, we turn to our
Father because His wisdom exceeds experience, His purpose exceeds our plans, and His
power exceeds our strength.
Prayer Is Relationship
Scripture tells us to bring requests to God specifically and expectantly, but prayer is more
than presenting needs to heaven. It is communion with God.
Because Christ reconciled us to the Father, the veil is torn and we have been brought
near. We worship, confess, listen, rejoice, lament, intercede, and surrender. Sometimes
we have many words; grief or weariness may leave us with few. God does not measure
vocabulary. He welcomes His children.
Some people believe they cannot pray because they do not sound like someone at church
whose voice changes and King James English suddenly appears. If that is how you pray,
pray on. But prayer is not a performance. Talk to your Father. We do not pray to
persuade God to care; we pray because He already does.

problem to change. There is nothing wrong with asking God to move mountains, heal,

We were never meant to walk alone. Culture admires independence, but Christianity is
filled with “one another”: love, encourage, forgive, serve, teach, pray for, confess to, and

God saves us individually but does not leave us isolated. He joins us to Christ and, in

More Than People in the Same Room
Gathering for worship, Scripture, prayer, and encouragement matters deeply. Yet sharing

Personal devotion is essential, but gathered worship offers something solitude cannot
reproduce. I have entered worship carrying a weight and heard someone sing with
strength I did not possess. Their faith helped carry mine.
Worship offered during trial becomes testimony: God is faithful. Christ is worthy. I am
still here. We gather because God deserves the praise of His people, and in His wisdom
our worship also strengthens one another. Sometimes I sing because I am strong;
sometimes because you are singing beside me.

Worship That Transforms
“God is spirit, and his worshipers must worship in the Spirit and in truth.” — John 4:24,

Worship Begins with God
Worship does not begin with our favorite song, our preferred style, the musicians, the
preacher, or even the mood we happen to be in when we arrive.
Worship begins with God.
It begins with who He is and what He has done. He is our Creator and Sustainer. He is
holy, faithful, merciful, and worthy. He loved us while we were still sinners, gave His Son
for our salvation, raised Him from the dead, and brought us into His family by grace.
We do not gather to make God worthy of worship.
We gather because He already is.
That truth protects us from turning worship into a matter of personal taste. Styles
change. Songs change. Instruments change. One generation may be carried by a hymn it
has sung for fifty years, while another is learning to praise God through a song written
last month. The important question is not simply, “Did I like it?” The better question is,

Worship Shapes the Heart
What we worship eventually shapes what we become.

Him. As we adore Him, our desires are reordered. As we confess our sins, humility grows.
As we remember His grace, gratitude deepens. As we hear His Word, faith is
strengthened. As we surrender again to His Lordship, obedience becomes our joyful
response.
The Holy Spirit uses worship to form the life of the disciple.
This means the purpose of worship is not merely to make us feel better for a little while.
Thank God, worship often encourages us. It lifts weary hearts, renews hope, and reminds
us that our circumstances are not greater than our God. But encouragement is not the
end of worship.
Transformation is.
We can sing loudly on Sunday and still live stubbornly on Monday. Volume is not the
same as surrender. A worship experience that moves our emotions but never reaches our
attitudes, relationships, decisions, and obedience has not yet completed its work in us.
Every gathering should leave us more devoted to Christ than when we arrived.
We do not gather merely to be inspired.
We gather to be transformed.

The Mission Belongs to All of Us
The mission of Cornerstone Fellowship Church is clear: “Working together, we exist to

Protecting the Culture
Stand firm. Let nothing move you. Always give yourselves fully to the work of the Lord. -

Every Church Has a Culture
Every church has a culture, whether it has been intentionally cultivated or simply allowed
to develop. Culture is more than the atmosphere in a room or the style of a worship
service. It is the shared understanding of how we live, serve, decide, disagree, celebrate,
and carry the mission together.
In simple terms, culture is what feels normal. It quietly answers the question, How do we
do life together here? A policy may explain what a church permits, but culture teaches
what the church actually values. People learn those values by watching what leaders
emphasize, what members celebrate, what behavior is corrected, and what conduct is
repeatedly overlooked.
At Cornerstone, our culture has been formed over more than thirty years of worship,
prayer, teaching, service, fellowship, hardship, forgiveness, and faith. It is not perfect
because we are not perfect. Yet God has patiently taught us convictions worth protecting:
Christ belongs at the center, Scripture speaks with authority, discipleship requires
obedience, people matter more than positions, and leadership exists to serve.
Culture is one of God's great gifts to a local church, but it is also one of our greatest
stewardships. Every generation receives something it did not create by itself and then
decides, through daily choices, what the next generation will inherit.
Culture Is Formed Through Repetition
Healthy culture is rarely created by one dramatic moment. It grows through ordinary
actions repeated until they become part of a congregation's character. A sermon is
prepared faithfully, a visitor is welcomed warmly, a child is treated with dignity, a
grieving family receives care, a conflict is handled honestly, and a leader gives credit
rather than seeking it.
None of these moments may appear historic when they occur. Taken together, however,
they teach people what kind of church we are becoming. Culture is not built by our
intentions alone; it is built by what we repeatedly practice.
The same is true in the wrong direction. One unchecked attitude, one habit of gossip, one
leader who is allowed to mistreat people, or one ministry that continually works against
the mission can slowly reshape what feels normal. What we repeatedly tolerate, we
eventually teach.
This is why small decisions matter. Every choice either strengthens the culture we say we
value or quietly weakens it.
We Protect Convictions, Not Preferences
Protecting culture does not mean resisting every change. A church can preserve familiar
methods so fiercely that it loses the mission those methods were meant to serve. We are
not called to keep everything exactly as it has always been; we are called to remain
faithful to Christ.

CHAPTER SEVENTEEN
Succession as Stewardship
“And the things you have heard me say in the presence of many witnesses entrust to
reliable people who will also be qualified to teach others.” — 2 Timothy 2:2, NIV
Every Leader Serves for a Season
Every Christian leader is temporary. That truth may sound unsettling at first, especially
when a pastor or ministry leader has served faithfully for many years. Yet it is not a
reason for fear. It is one of the clearest reminders that Jesus Christ alone is the Head of
His Church.
Pastors, deacons, teachers, ministry leaders, musicians, and faithful servants all receive
an assignment for a season. We should value every season God gives, but we must never
confuse a long assignment with permanent ownership. The church belongs to Christ, not
to the person currently carrying the greatest responsibility.
This conviction brings humility and freedom. It keeps leaders from building personal
kingdoms and congregations from placing upon a leader the weight only Jesus can bear.
We can honor those who serve without making them the center or believing God's work
depends entirely upon them.
One day every responsibility we hold will pass into other hands. Faithful stewardship
means serving well while it is ours and preparing others to serve when it is no longer
ours.
Succession Begins Long Before Transition
Succession is often treated as a difficult conversation to begin when a leader announces a
retirement, accepts another calling, or can no longer continue. By then, however, a
church may already be late. Healthy succession does not begin with a vacancy. It begins
with discipleship.
Every time we teach Scripture, invite a younger believer into a conversation, explain a
decision, or entrust meaningful responsibility to another person, we are preparing the
future. Parents who help children follow Jesus, teachers who develop other teachers, and
ministry leaders who make room for new servants are all participating in succession.
This is not merely an organizational strategy. It is the pattern Paul gave Timothy: receive
truth faithfully, live it openly, and entrust it to reliable people who will teach others. The
Gospel travels from one generation to another through people who understand that what
they have received was never meant to stop with them.
At Cornerstone, succession should be part of our normal life together. We should not wait
until a transition becomes urgent before asking who is growing, who is faithful, who is
teachable, and whom God may be preparing. The strongest transitions are usually the
fruit of years of quiet investment.
We Prepare People, Not Replacements
There is a difference between preparing people and searching for replacements. A
replacement is often expected to preserve a position exactly as it has been. A developing
leader is encouraged to become the person God has called and gifted that individual to
be.

If we only release responsibility when we are exhausted or unavailable, we have missed

Measuring Faithfulness
“Now it is required that those who have been given a trust must prove faithful.” — 1
Corinthians 4:2, NIV
Success Must Be Measured Carefully
Every church measures something. We count attendance, review budgets, track
participation, and notice whether ministries are growing or declining. Churches may also
measure online engagement and community reach. None of this is inherently wrong.
Good information can help faithful leaders make wise decisions.
The danger comes when a useful measurement becomes our definition of success. A full
room can encourage us, but it cannot tell us whether hearts are being transformed. A
balanced budget may reflect careful stewardship, but it cannot tell us whether generosity
is growing. A busy calendar proves that activity is occurring; it does not prove that
disciples are maturing.
At Cornerstone, we measure what helps us serve the mission Christ has entrusted to us.
Numbers tell part of the story, but not all of it. We ask not only, “Are more people
coming?” but also, “Are people becoming more like Jesus?” We ask not only, “Is this
ministry active?” but also, “Is it helping people take faithful steps with Christ?”
Measurement is a servant, not a master. It should help us see clearly, give thanks
honestly, correct wisely, and steward faithfully.
We count because people matter, not to make the church appear important. Every
number represents a person, responsibility, or resource entrusted to us. Changes in
participation, baptisms, or giving should lead us to ask thoughtful questions and respond
faithfully. Yet numbers require humility. Growth does not always mean health, and
decline does not always mean failure. One ministry may become smaller because it sent
people into service; another may grow while neglecting discipleship. Information
becomes wisdom only when considered prayerfully and in the light of Scripture.
Faithfulness Before Fruitfulness
Fruit matters. Jesus taught that His disciples would bear fruit, and a church should desire
changed lives, strengthened families, developed leaders, and people brought to faith. We
must never use faithfulness as an excuse for carelessness or a refusal to examine our
ministry.
Yet Scripture also teaches that lasting fruit grows from abiding in Christ. We plant and
water, but God gives the increase. Our responsibility is to obey faithfully, serve diligently,
and trust Him with outcomes we cannot manufacture.
This protects the church from manipulation. We do not pressure people into decisions to
report impressive results, change Scripture's message to gain approval, or treat another
congregation as
competition. Comparison makes us proud when our numbers look larger and discouraged
when they do not. Neither helps us follow Jesus.
Faithfulness asks a better question: Are we obeying what Christ has called us to do with
the people, gifts, opportunities, and resources He has placed in our hands? If we can

What We Celebrate Reveals What We Value
People learn what a church values by listening to what it celebrates. If the loudest
applause is reserved for visible growth, large gifts, and public leadership, we may teach
that quiet faithfulness matters less. God's Kingdom often advances through obedience
that never appears in a report.
At Cornerstone, we rejoice when someone places faith in Jesus Christ and publicly
identifies with Him through baptism. We celebrate when a believer returns to fellowship,
a marriage begins healing, a child learns to trust God, or a family discovers the joy of

person follow Jesus.
We also celebrate the servant who arrives early, the person who prays without

someone others missed. These acts reveal the character of Christ being formed among
His people.
Celebration teaches culture. When we tell these stories, we remind one another that
heaven's values are often different from the world's. What matters most is not being
noticed. It is being faithful.
Questions Worth Asking
Every ministry should periodically ask why it exists, whom it serves, and whether it is
helping fulfill the mission. These conversations should not wait until something fails.
Regular evaluation is healthy stewardship.
We can ask whether people are treasuring Jesus more deeply and understanding
Scripture more clearly. Are they learning to pray, obey, forgive, give, serve, and walk by
faith? Are relationships becoming healthier? Are members discovering their gifts? Are
new leaders emerging? Are children and students being formed as disciples? Are people
coming to faith, and are new believers being helped to grow?
We should ask practical questions. Is the ministry using time and money wisely? Are its
leaders healthy? Does it strengthen the whole church or compete for attention and
resources? Is it meeting a real need, or are we maintaining it because it has always
existed?
Honest questions are not attacks upon faithful servants. They help connect effort to a
clear purpose. Evaluation requires prayer, gratitude, reliable information, and willingness
to change. The goal is not to assign blame but to become more faithful.
Measuring Spiritual Health
Spiritual health cannot always be charted, but it can be observed. Healthy churches
develop recognizable patterns: joy in worship, hunger for God's Word, dependence upon
prayer, generosity, and love. Leaders serve humbly. Members remain faithful during
trials. People repent, forgive, reconcile, and expect God to remain at work.
These qualities are recognized over time. One joyful service does not prove health, just as
one difficult season does not prove failure. We look for direction and consistency. Are we
becoming more prayerful? Are conflicts handled more biblically? Are members moving

treat one another.
The healthiest church is not the one with no problems. It is the one that keeps bringing

Measuring Leadership
Leadership should never be evaluated by visibility alone. A gifted communicator may
gather attention while lacking the character required for long-term trust. Another person
may serve far from the platform while steadily strengthening people and ministries.
Influence matters, but Christlike influence matters more.

leader strengthen unity, develop others, and steward authority responsibly?
We also ask whether leaders are healthy. Constant exhaustion is not proof of devotion.
Neglected families, unaddressed sin, and unsustainable workloads harm both the leader
and church. Faithful leadership includes rest, shared responsibility, appropriate
boundaries, and receiving care.
The goal of evaluation is not perfection. Every leader is still being formed. We are looking
for humility, teachability, faithfulness, and growth. A healthy leader does not need to
know everything, but must remain willing to learn.
Measuring the Next Generation

Are they learning Scripture, discovering prayer, and developing personal trust in Jesus
rather than borrowing the faith of adults? Can they ask honest questions? Are they
building relationships with mature believers, discovering gifts, serving meaningfully, and
learning that following Christ reaches every part of life?
We must strengthen parents and caregivers as disciple-makers. The church supports
children's spiritual formation but cannot replace the daily influence of home. Healthy
ministry equips families, welcomes those whose homes are spiritually complicated, and
surrounds young people with trustworthy believers who model Christ.
A large youth event may encourage us, but the deeper question is what remains
afterward. If the next generation treasures Jesus, loves His Church, serves others, and

and Christlike character more than recognition. Our goal is not merely a growing church
but a growing people who treasure Jesus, serve one another, and make disciples through
His love. When our service is complete, the final measure will not be how impressive our
ministries appeared. We long to hear Jesus say, “Well done, good and faithful servant.”
